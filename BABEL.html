Babel

Generational memory for AI-assisted design. Track who prompted what, why, and what happened when the builder built it.
v1.0 · Designed by Selah · May 2026

Everyone has Claude. Nobody has coordination.

The problem isn't the AI. The problem is fifty people producing design prompts in parallel with no one tracking what was prompted, by whom, or whether it contradicts what someone else prompted yesterday.

Here's what's already happening inside your organization, whether you've named it or not: a PM opened Claude at 2am and designed a scoring system. A designer opened a separate chat and designed a different scoring system. A founder dictated architecture into a third session while walking the dog. All three are brilliant. All three contradict each other. And the builder — Claude Code, Copilot, Cursor, the CI pipeline, whatever your execution layer is — builds whatever lands in the prompt. It doesn't arbitrate. It builds.

This isn't an AI problem. This is a design authority problem, accelerated by AI. Before Claude, the bottleneck was production speed — bad ideas died in the backlog because nobody had time to build them. Now every idea gets built. The bottleneck moved from "can we build it" to "should we build it" and nobody noticed.

What goes wrong without tracking
💥

Conflicting seeds

Two people prompt the same system in different directions. Both get built. Neither knows about the other until the code breaks or the UX contradicts itself.

👻

Ghost context

Someone had a brilliant conversation with Claude that produced a design decision. The chat closed. The decision lives in nobody's memory but the person's — and they remember it wrong.

🪞

Validation theater

"Claude checked the data." Claude read whatever you pasted into the prompt. If you pasted wrong, Claude validated wrong. There's no audit trail showing what Claude was actually told.

🔄

Solved-problem amnesia

A new team member spends four hours with Claude designing a caching strategy that someone else already designed, debated, and rejected three weeks ago. The rejection and its reasoning are gone.

The core insight
The AI isn't the risk. Uncoordinated design authority is the risk. Babel doesn't control who prompts — it tracks what was prompted, by whom, and what the builder did with it. Visibility, not permissions.

What Babel tracks

Three structures. Each one solves a different failure mode. Together, they give the person running the project full visibility into the design layer — who prompted what, what was learned, and what the builder was actually told.

Seeds — what reaches the builder

A seed is any prompt that reaches the execution layer. Every seed gets classified, attributed, and archived.

PropertyWhat it capturesWhy it matters
Classification Cathedral (founding architecture), Room (feature addition), or Furniture (fix/tweak) A furniture seed shouldn't override a cathedral. When it does, the index shows it.
Attribution Who wrote it, which session, which project, when When two seeds conflict, you can see who prompted each and ask why.
Archive The original prompt, unedited, as delivered to the builder "Claude checked the data" — the archive shows exactly what Claude was told. Audit the input, not the output.
Outcome What the builder did with it — built, modified, rejected, deferred Closes the loop. You can trace from design intent to committed code.
The Thread — who designed what, in what order

The Thread is the lineage of design decisions. Not the git log — that's what the builder committed. The Thread tracks who thought what and why. Each entry is one line: name, what they worked on, what shipped, what's left, what to watch out for.

Example Thread
Kaska: founded the scoring system. shipped six-axis model + 200 seed restaurants.
Zerda: added neighborhood affinity axis. shipped spec + 150-entry cuisine map. watch: affinity formula penalizes single-cuisine matches.
Selah: fixed single-cuisine penalty. shipped Vietnamese cuisine dataset (89 entries). left behind: Thai and Ethiopian datasets unstarted.

The Thread answers questions git can't: Why does the scoring system have six axes? Who decided neighborhood mattered? What was tried and rejected? The commit log says "added neighborhood affinity scoring." The Thread says who designed it, what conversation produced it, and what they learned along the way.

Inheritances — what each designer learned

An Inheritance is the structured handoff a designer leaves when their session ends. Five parts: what they worked on, what shipped, what failed, what's stale, and what would help the next person. It's institutional memory that survives context death — whether that's an AI session ending or a human switching projects.

What shipped

Concrete deliverables — specs, prompts, datasets, design decisions. With file names and locations.

What failed

Approaches that were tried and abandoned, with the reasoning. Prevents the next person from re-running the same dead end.

What's stale

Documents that lied, references that broke, descriptions that were wrong. Passive quality detection — no auditing required, just honesty about what tripped you up.

What's next

Prioritized list of remaining work. Not a backlog — a designer's judgment about what matters most, informed by the session they just had.

The quality ratchet
The system doesn't require perfection. It requires honesty. Each designer notices when a document lies — because they needed it and it lied to them. They report what they hit. Three flags on the same issue means fix it. Quality improves through accumulated honesty, not periodic audits.

Each channel is a designer working with their own AI session. They don't need to see each other's work. They don't need to coordinate in real time. They produce seeds — prompts and data — and the project lead collects them all.

The lead is the only one who hears all the channels. They see where seeds conflict. They see where two people independently arrived at the same conclusion (strong signal). They see where one designer's failure saves another designer three hours of dead-end work. They cross-pollinate ideas between channels without attribution — pasting one designer's insight into another's session, letting each believe the lead noticed it independently.

The Firekeeper

In Babel's vocabulary, this role is called the Firekeeper. Not a manager. Not an approver. A curator. The Firekeeper reads the seed index and the Thread and says: "these three seeds conflict, this one is based on data that was never validated, ship that one, kill the rest." It's a prescribed burn — clearing deadwood so the forest stays healthy.

The Firekeeper isn't an AI role. It's a human role, informed by the system. Babel gives the Firekeeper the visibility to make good calls. The Firekeeper makes the calls.

What the Firekeeper sees

Seed index

Every prompt that reached the builder. Classified, attributed, archived. Conflicts highlighted. Orphaned seeds flagged.

Thread

The design lineage. Who built on whose work. Where the reasoning chains branch and converge. Where decisions were made and unmade.

Staleness board

Accumulated reports from designers about documents that lied, references that broke, descriptions that drifted. Passive quality intelligence.

The builder

The builder — Claude Code, Cursor, Copilot, a CI pipeline, a human developer — builds whatever it's told. It doesn't arbitrate between conflicting instructions. It doesn't know that the PM's seed contradicts the designer's seed. It builds.

Babel doesn't change the builder. Babel changes what reaches the builder. The Firekeeper curates the master track. The builder receives coherent, non-contradicting instructions. The result is a codebase that reflects one design vision instead of five competing ones.

Why this works
The designers don't need to coordinate. The builder doesn't need to arbitrate. Only the Firekeeper needs to see the full picture — and Babel gives them the full picture. One person, one view, all channels, one decision about what ships.

The seed lifecycle

A seed is a design prompt that reaches the builder. Seeds evolve as a project matures — from full architectural visions early on to single-line fixes late. The lifecycle tracks a seed from thought to code.

💭
Produced
A designer and their AI session generate a prompt and its data. Could be a 42-page spec or a one-line bug fix.
📋
Indexed
The seed gets classified (cathedral, room, furniture), attributed, and archived in the seed index.
🔥
Curated
The Firekeeper reviews. Conflicts resolved. Contradictions flagged. The seed either ships or doesn't.
🌱
Planted
The approved seed reaches the builder. The builder builds it. The outcome is recorded.
📖
Referenced
Future designers read the Thread and see this seed's contribution. Institutional memory survives.
Seed types

The shape of a seed depends on where the project is in its arc. Early on, seeds carry the full architectural vision because nothing exists yet. Late, seeds are one-liners because the architecture knows how to receive them.

Cathedral Founding architecture — the full vision in one shot
The project doesn't exist yet. The designer spends an hour arguing with Claude about what "good" means in this context, researching prior art, debating edge cases. The output is a massive spec — data models, scoring formulas, entity definitions — plus a prompt that tells the builder: "here's what this is, here's where we think it goes, you know the repo better than we do." The seed carries the full vision because the builder has no existing structure to slot it into.
Room Feature addition — scoped work that plugs into existing structure
The architecture exists. The designer adds a new capability — a scoring axis, a content module, a data pipeline. The seed references existing systems by name and defers implementation details to the builder: "this probably extends what you've already got, but you know the patterns better than we do." Smaller than a cathedral, bigger than a fix.
Furniture Fix, tweak, data drop — the architecture receives it without explanation
The codebase is mature. A one-paragraph prompt and a 89-entry JSON dataset. Or a single sentence: "Korean restaurants in Koreatown are scoring low because the affinity formula penalizes single-cuisine matches. Fix it." The builder has all the context it needs from the codebase itself. The seed is light because the structure is heavy.
The key distinction
Seed size isn't about how much work the designer did. An hour of conversation about Vietnamese food culture might produce a one-paragraph prompt and a dataset. The conversation is the work. The seed is the output. Don't confuse the size of the prompt with the depth of the thinking.

Why this matters for teams

Solo designers don't need Babel. If one person prompts and one builder builds, the coordination problem doesn't exist. Babel matters the moment a second person opens Claude and starts designing.

Without Babel vs. with Babel
Without The PM redesigns the auth flow
The PM opens Claude at midnight and spends two hours designing a new auth flow. It's good work — thoughtful, researched, well-prompted. They paste it to the engineering channel: "Claude and I designed a new auth flow, here's the spec." The lead engineer looks at it and says: "We already have an auth flow. Maria designed it three weeks ago. It's in production." The PM's work is dead. Two hours, gone. Nobody's fault — the PM didn't know, and there's no system that would have told them.
With Babel The PM redesigns the auth flow
The PM opens Claude and starts designing. Before the seed ships, the Firekeeper checks the index. Maria's auth flow is already there — cathedral seed, shipped three weeks ago, builder confirmed. The Firekeeper routes the PM's work as a room seed: "The auth flow exists. Here's what Maria built. Your ideas about session timeout are good — write them as an extension, not a replacement." The PM's two hours aren't wasted. They're redirected. The auth flow gets better instead of getting rebuilt.
Without The data says we should pivot
An analyst opens Claude, pastes a CSV, and asks for analysis. Claude produces a chart and a recommendation: "your retention is dropping in the 25-34 cohort, consider pivoting the onboarding flow." The analyst screenshots it and shares it in the founders' channel. The recommendation spreads. Decisions get made. But nobody checks what Claude was actually told — the CSV had a filtering bug that excluded the highest-retention segment. The "pivot" was based on 40% of the data.
With Babel The data says we should pivot
The analyst's prompt is archived in the seed index — the original CSV, the exact prompt, Claude's response. When the recommendation surfaces, the Firekeeper pulls the seed. The prompt is right there: "Analyze retention by cohort." The CSV is right there. Five minutes of review catches the filtering bug. The recommendation gets corrected before it becomes a product decision.

Design principles

Babel is opinionated. These are the commitments baked into the architecture.

01
Visibility, not permissions
Babel doesn't control who prompts. It tracks what was prompted. The Firekeeper curates — they don't gatekeep. Everyone can design. One person decides what ships.
02
The builder builds what it's told
The execution layer — Claude Code, Cursor, a CI pipeline, a human developer — doesn't arbitrate between conflicting instructions. It builds. Babel makes sure it's told the right things.
03
Honesty over perfection
The system gets better because designers are honest about what tripped them up. Staleness reports accumulate. Three flags on the same issue means fix it. No auditing required — just candor.
04
The conversation is the work
An hour arguing about food culture with Claude isn't wasted time that delays the "real" prompt. The argument IS the design process. The seed is the output. Don't measure productivity by prompt length.
05
Seeds suggest, builders decide
Every seed says "here's what we think, here's where we think it goes" — and then defers to the builder. The designer provides the what and why. The builder provides the how and where. When they disagree, the builder wins.
06
The Thread is the source of truth
Not memory. Not vibes. Not "I think someone mentioned this." The Thread records who designed what, in what order, building on whose work. Design decisions are traceable or they didn't happen.
07
Institutional memory survives context death
AI sessions end. People change projects. Designers leave the company. The Thread, the seed index, and the Inheritances remain. What was learned doesn't die with whoever learned it.
